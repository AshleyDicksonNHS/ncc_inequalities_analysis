1 Methods

1.1 Study Population

Primary analysis: 476,475 hospital births in England during financial year 2023/24 with complete data on NCC admission, gestation, ethnicity, IMD quintile, and delivery trust.

Sensitivity analysis: 218,886 births (45.9% of primary sample) with additional complete data on birth weight and Apgar 5-minute score.

The cohort is defined by birth in 2023/24, not by NCC activity in 2023/24. Babies enter on the basis of their MSDS birth record (the financial year is derived from year and month of birth); a baby born before April 2023 who continued a neonatal stay into the study year is not included. Critical-care linkage then looks outward from each birth — and is permitted to reach about 30 days into 2024/25 — so that NCC admissions for babies born late in March 2024 are not under-counted; but the linkage never pulls earlier-born babies into the cohort.

1.2 Outcome definition (NCC_Admitted)

The outcome flags whether a baby has care recorded as Neonatal Critical Care in either of the two available administrative sources: MSDS MSD402 (provider-submitted neonatal admission records) or PbR-CC (the Payment-by-Results critical-care daily extract, filtered to CC_Type = 'NCC'). A baby is NCC_Admitted = 1 if they appear in either source. The two sources do not agree perfectly: the CC source records roughly 3.8 times more NCC admissions than MSDS alone, and submission completeness for both sources varies between providers. The outcome should therefore be read as “received care recorded as NCC” rather than a clinical judgement that a baby needed a critical-care cot.

1.3 Cohort filter and deprivation vintage

Two further constraints apply to the primary cohort:

  • Hospital births only. The cohort is restricted to babies whose mother has a recorded MSD301 hospital labour-delivery spell (Mother_Admission_Date IS NOT NULL). This is consistent with the study’s “hospital births in England” frame, but it excludes home births and any births where the labour record did not link in MSDS — the latter is not random across providers.
  • IMD 2015 vintage. The deprivation field used (IMD_Decile_2015) is derived from the 2015 release of the English Indices of Deprivation. IMD 2019 is now the current vintage, and a 2025 release is anticipated. Some areas have reclassified noticeably since 2015. Findings should be interpreted as applying to the 2015 deprivation classification of the mother’s recorded address.

1.4 Variable selection

This is a causal estimation problem rather than a prediction problem, and that distinction governs the approach to selecting variables. The aim is not to predict NCC admission as accurately as possible; it is to estimate the association of ethnicity and deprivation with admission conditional on clinical need. Each candidate variable is therefore judged first on its causal role, and only then on whether it is usable:

  • Causal role. A variable is an exposure of interest (ethnicity, IMD — the inequalities being measured), a confounder of need (clinical case-mix that must be adjusted for so that “conditional on need” is meaningful), a mediator (on the causal pathway from exposure to admission, which must not be adjusted for if the total effect is wanted — the “Table 2 fallacy”), or a collider (adjusting for which would introduce bias). Confounders are candidates for adjustment; mediators and colliders are left out, however predictive they are.
  • Clinical relevance. Among confounders, how strongly the variable drives genuine NCC need.
  • Completeness. Whether it is recorded for enough of the cohort to use without shrinking or biasing the sample. A near-complete weak confounder can be preferable to a strong but half-missing one — the gestation-versus-birth-weight trade-off below.
  • Collinearity. Correlation among the adjustment variables inflates the variance of those coefficients, but does not bias the ethnicity/IMD estimates unless a covariate is collinear with ethnicity or IMD. Correlated clinical measures are a reason for parsimony, not a threat to the headline estimates.

1.4.1 Why selection is not data-driven (stepwise / LASSO)

Algorithmic selection — stepwise regression, LASSO, and similar — optimises predictive fit and is blind to causal role. Such a procedure would happily adjust for a mediator (biasing the exposure estimate toward the null), or drop a true confounder because it is collinear with another. For a causal access question this is the wrong tool: it answers “what predicts admission?” when we are asking “is admission equitable conditional on need?”. Selection here is therefore pre-specified on clinical and causal grounds by design. Where robustness to the adjustment set matters, the appropriate check is not a data-driven search but a small set of pre-specified alternative specifications (see Recommendations in the Findings report).

In short, the primary model deliberately carries the need signal on gestation alone — the one near-complete clinical confounder — adds the two exposures of interest, and leaves the trust structure to the random intercept. The sensitivity model adds birth weight and Apgar where they are observed. The remaining candidates are excluded either by causal role (mediators, colliders, and components of the exposure construct) or by data availability (factors awaiting a dataset rebuild), and are picked up as future work rather than omitted for convenience.

1.5 Statistical Approach

We fitted three models:

  1. Model 1 (Fixed effects only): Standard logistic regression adjusting for gestation, ethnicity, and deprivation. Retained as a reference point; estimates are in Technical Details.

  2. Model 2 (Mixed effects, gestation only): Hierarchical logistic regression with random intercepts for delivery trust, adjusting only for gestation among clinical covariates \[\text{logit}(P[\text{NCC}]) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{Gestation} + \beta_2 \text{Ethnicity} + \beta_3 \text{IMD} + u_j\] where \(u_j \sim N(0, \sigma^2_u)\) represents the random effect for delivery trust \(j\). Reported because it uses the maximum sample size (gestation is near-completely observed).

  3. Model 3 (Mixed effects, full clinical adjustment): Adds birth weight and Apgar 5-minute score to Model 2’s specification \[\text{logit}(P[\text{NCC}]) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{Gestation} + \beta_2 \text{BirthWeight} + \beta_3 \text{Apgar} + \beta_4 \text{Ethnicity} + \beta_5 \text{IMD} + u_j\]

Conceptually Model 3 is the preferred specification — it adjusts for the clinical case-mix that drives NCC need rather than relying on gestation alone — and we lead the interpretation with its results. Model 2 is reported in parallel because of its larger sample (gestation is observed for nearly all births, whereas birth weight and Apgar are observed for 45.9% of the primary sample). Where the two models disagree (notably for Pakistani and Indian ethnic groups), the disagreement itself is informative and is discussed below.

1.6 Birth Weight Data Source

The sensitivity model uses birth weight from MSDS only (Birth_Weight_Grams), not the combined MSDS+CC variable. This avoids differential missingness bias: the CC data has 94% birth weight completeness for NCC babies vs 46% for non-NCC babies, which would artificially inflate NCC rates in complete cases.

1.7 Random-effect Grouping

The hierarchical models cluster babies at the level of the submitting NHS trust (3-letter provider code), not the individual delivery site (5-letter code). Two practical considerations drove this:

  1. Coverage. MSDS populates the site-of-delivery field (Org_Site_ID_Actual_Place_Delivery) for ~83% of babies; the remaining ~17% have no site recorded but do have a submitting-trust code (OrgCodeProvider) populated for 100% of records. The no-site cohort is concentrated in 15 large multi-site trusts and is not random with respect to outcome (NCC rate 14.2% vs 11.5% in the with-site cohort), ethnicity (more Pakistani, fewer Indian) or deprivation. Excluding it on a missing-site filter would bias the model.

  2. Hierarchy. Site codes (e.g. RYJ01) sit administratively inside trust codes (e.g. RYJ). Treating “RYJ01” babies and “RYJ-trust-only” babies as independent random-effect groups would double-count trust-level variance and break the exchangeability assumption. Clustering at trust level for everyone keeps a single consistent rung.

The grouping variable is constructed as Delivery_Trust = coalesce(LEFT(Delivery_Site_Code, 3), Submitting_Org_Code), then used as the random-intercept term (1 | Delivery_Trust). For multi-site trusts (Imperial, Leicester, Barts, etc.), within-trust between-site variation is folded into the residual rather than the random effect; the reported ICC is therefore a slightly conservative estimate of between-hospital variation.

1.7.1 Why mixed effects?

A standard regression would treat all 500,000+ babies as independent draws from one giant, uniform hospital. They are not: babies are clustered within trusts, and the baseline likelihood of NCC admission varies widely between trusts — through clinical thresholds, local capacity and reporting practice. Ignoring that clustering has two costs. It understates uncertainty (babies within a hospital partly repeat the same hospital-level information), and it can generate false findings: if an ethnic group happens to be concentrated around hospitals that admit (or report) more, a standard model would attribute the hospital’s behaviour to the group.

The mixed-effects model fixes this by giving each trust its own baseline admission rate (a “random intercept”), with the trust baselines treated as draws from a common distribution. The patient-level effects — ethnicity, deprivation, clinical need — are then estimated with the hospital baseline held constant. So when we report that the most deprived quintile has 9.4% higher odds of admission, it means: for two clinically identical babies born in the same trust, the baby from the most deprived area is 9.4% more likely to be admitted.

2.1.4 Funnel Plot: Case-mix-adjusted Admission Rates

The review suggested a funnel plot — as used in the GIRFT programme — as an alternative to the caterpillar plot above. The two show the same trust-level variation but answer slightly different questions. The caterpillar ranks trusts by their estimated random effect; the funnel asks, more directly, which trusts admit at a rate that case-mix alone cannot explain?

Each trust is plotted against the number of NCC admissions expected given the case-mix of the babies born there (gestation, ethnicity and deprivation), using the fixed-effects part of the primary model, i.e. with the trust’s own random effect removed. The horizontal line is the national crude NCC rate across the trusts plotted (~12%; this is computed on the complete-case modelling sample used for the funnel and so sits marginally below the ~13% rate for the full birth cohort reported elsewhere). The curved lines are 95% and 99.8% control limits. A trust inside the funnel is varying no more than chance would predict; a trust outside the 99.8% (≈3-sigma) limit is a statistical outlier. Because the genuine between-trust variation is large (the same signal as the ICC), the limits are widened to account for this overdispersion (see the method note below), so that only the most extreme trusts are flagged rather than the majority.

An extreme position can reflect genuine practice variation, but equally a tertiary referral centre (which legitimately admits more), or a provider whose MSDS / PbR-CC submission is out of step with peers. The table below flags each 99.8% outlier with its neonatal unit type (NICU units are tertiary and expected to sit high) and its MSDS source share — the proportion of the trust’s NCC babies that appear in the MSDS neonatal record rather than only in the CC extract. A low share points to under-submission in MSDS and heavier reliance on the CC source: a data-quality signal rather than a clinical one. Note, though, that a low share changes where an admission is recorded, not whether it is counted — the combined admission flag picks a baby up from either source — so MSDS under-submission cannot by itself inflate a trust’s admission rate (see the diagnostic below).

2.1.4.1 Missing records, missing fields, or a broader numerator?

NoteThe three mechanisms tested, and why two are ruled out (click to expand)

Three data mechanisms could place a trust spuriously outside the funnel:

  1. Record-level completeness: the trust submits few MSDS birth records, so its denominator is a small, unrepresentative slice of its true delivery population.
  2. Field-level completeness: births are submitted, but the model fields (gestation, ethnicity, IMD) are missing, so the complete-case sample keeps a selected, likely sicker and better-documented, subset of its births.
  3. Numerator breadth: the denominator is sound, but the trust’s PbR-CC return counts more of its neonatal unit activity as “NCC” days than peers’ returns do.

The table rules out the first two mechanisms for all four outliers:

  • Their birth records are present in expected volumes: each outlier trust submitted between roughly 4,900 and 10,800 births — all above the median trust (~3,700) — in line with their real delivery-service size. This is nothing like a missing-denominator pattern.
  • Their field completeness is unremarkable: 93–99% of their births carry all three model fields (nationally 94%), and each trust’s crude NCC rate is essentially unchanged when restricted to the model-usable subset. Complete-case selection is not what lifts them out of the funnel.
  • A third explanation also fails on arithmetic: referral activity cannot raise a trust’s position in a delivery-cohort funnel. The funnel counts babies born at each trust, and over 99% of the NCC recorded for those babies was provided by the delivery trust itself. Transfers into a tertiary unit never enter its numerator here.

What remains — and what the diagnostic table above shows — is the third data mechanism. At the outlier trusts, 21–40% of term (≥37-week) babies carry an NCC record, against ~7% everywhere else, while their preterm birth shares are unremarkable (7.7–8.7%, national 7.9%). Their admitted babies are correspondingly term-heavy and their recorded stays short; at three of the four trusts the admissions are also recorded almost entirely in the CC extract. A true critical-care rate of one in four term births is not clinically plausible; the far more likely reading is that these providers return a broader span of neonatal unit activity — special or transitional care, possibly routine observation — as NCC critical-care days in PbR-CC. This extract cannot fully separate “admits more term babies to the unit” (practice) from “codes more unit days as critical care” (recording), because the care-level fields that would settle it (Level 2/3 days, unit function) are unpopulated here; but the recording component is likely dominant.

Two consequences follow. First, the NICU-vs-LNU split above no longer holds: the near-zero MSDS source share at the two NICU outliers is a genuine reporting gap, but it cannot inflate their rate, and all four outliers share the same term-heavy, short-stay signature. Second, the practical follow-up changes: the first question for these trusts concerns what unit activity their PbR-CC return counts as an NCC day — a data-definition conversation before a clinical-practice one. This also sharpens the caution attached to the ICC throughout this report: a material share of the apparent between-trust variation is reporting breadth, not admission practice. (Trust identities are held internally and omitted from this published version.)

NoteHow the funnel is built (click to expand)

Expected admissions are the population-average (case-mix only) predictions from the primary model with the trust random effect removed (predict(..., re.form = NA)), aggregated per trust and indirectly standardised so the national observed/expected ratio is 1. Control limits use the standard funnel formulation (Spiegelhalter, Statistics in Medicine, 2005): on the standardised-ratio scale the limits are \(1 \pm z\sqrt{\phi}/\sqrt{E}\), shown here multiplied by the national crude rate.

Because between-trust variation here is far larger than binomial sampling noise (the same signal as the ICC), the limits include a multiplicative overdispersion factor \(\phi\), estimated from the winsorised standardised residuals so that a handful of extreme trusts do not themselves inflate it. Without this adjustment almost every trust would fall outside the limits — a true reflection of the large ICC, but useless for isolating individual outliers. Even so, the same data-quality caveats apply as to the ICC: MSDS / PbR-CC submission completeness varies between providers, so outliers should be read as flags for follow-up, not as confirmed differences in admission practice. The completeness diagnostic above shows how to separate these readings — for the current outliers the evidence points to numerator breadth in the PbR-CC return rather than either missing records or missing fields.

3 Interpretation

3.1 Clinical Factors

Gestation is by far the strongest predictor of NCC admission: each additional week reduces the odds by approximately 38%. Preterm babies are correspondingly more likely to be admitted, confirming that the model is capturing genuine clinical need.

3.2 Ethnicity

After adjusting for gestation and comparing within trusts, most ethnic groups sit within a few percent of parity with White British babies (full estimates in the Model 2 table and Figure 1). The Black group sits at near-exact parity; Pakistani and Chinese babies sit below parity in this model; Indian and Bangladeshi babies sit slightly above. How each estimate responds when birth weight and Apgar are added is the key to interpreting them:

Group Primary OR Sensitivity OR Interpretation
Pakistani 0.89 0.97 Moves towards parity — clinical factors explain much of the difference
Chinese 0.70 0.76 Still notably lower, at the boundary of nominal significance in the smaller sample
Indian 1.05 1.16 Increases further — higher access after clinical adjustment
Bangladeshi 1.06 1.17 Similar pattern to Indian
Black 1.04 1.04 Stable — consistent with equitable access

The Pakistani estimate is largely a case-mix finding: the lower odds in the primary model move to near-parity once birth weight and Apgar are adjusted for. The Indian and Bangladeshi estimates move further above parity, suggesting NCC use at least proportionate to clinical need. The differences among Indian, Pakistani and Bangladeshi babies after adjustment are the clearest demonstration of why this report uses disaggregated ethnicity coding.

The Chinese group is the largest unexplained deviation: around 24% lower odds remain after clinical adjustment (the BH-adjusted p-value in the Model 2 table is the appropriate test of evidence). Three broad explanations are consistent with this, and the data cannot separate them:

  • Unmeasured clinical need — Chinese babies have lower mean birth weight at term in UK data, and the cohort likely differs from the reference group in maternal age structure, parity, and rates of high-risk pregnancy; gestation alone, and a single linear birth-weight term in the sensitivity model, may not fully capture this.
  • Sample size — Chinese is one of the smaller ethnic groups in the cohort, so the estimate is less stable than the odds ratio alone suggests.
  • Data and access factors — two distinct possibilities the data cannot tell apart: genuine differences in care-seeking or antenatal engagement (including selective use of private antenatal care), and possible under-recording of NCC episodes in this group. Neither has direct evidence for or against it here.

Distinguishing these would need richer clinical covariates or direct engagement with providers (see Recommendations in the Findings report). The estimate should be treated as a question rather than a conclusion.

3.2.1 A broad, MBRRACE-comparable grouping

MBRRACE-UK reports present their headline ethnicity findings using broad groups (White, Mixed, Asian, Black, Other), and it is a fair question whether the ten-category grouping used here is more detailed than the data can support. To allow direct comparison, the primary model was refitted on the same 476,475 births with ethnicity collapsed to the five broad ONS groups; every other part of the model specification is unchanged. The broad grouping is an exact aggregation of the detailed one (each detailed category maps into exactly one broad group), so any difference between the two tables below is purely a consequence of the grouping choice.

At the broad level the picture is straightforward: the Asian or Asian British group sits at OR 1.00, the Black or Black British group at 1.05, and no broad group deviates dramatically from the White reference. This is a useful headline for comparison with MBRRACE-style reporting, and it confirms that no broad ethnic group faces large systematic under-admission at national level.

Figure 4 shows what the aggregation costs. The single broad Asian estimate is a weighted average of detailed groups whose estimates point in opposite directions — Pakistani below parity, Indian and Bangladeshi above it (a spread from 0.89 to 1.06) — and these are not noise: the sensitivity analysis showed they respond differently to clinical adjustment, as would be expected of real, distinct patterns of need and access. Likewise, the Chinese group — the largest single-group deviation in the analysis (OR 0.70) — is diluted into the broad “Other” category, where a 30% deficit attached to an identifiable group becomes a 9% deficit attached to a heterogeneous residual one. A model using only broad groups would have reported near-exact parity for Asian babies and a modest unattributable deficit for “Other”, losing the two most interpretable ethnicity findings in this report.

This is why the report leads with the detailed grouping and treats the broad grouping as a comparability view rather than the primary analysis. The multiple-testing cost of the extra detail is handled explicitly (Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment within the ethnicity family), and MBRRACE itself disaggregates beyond the broad groups in its extended tables where numbers allow. The recommendation that follows concerns reporting, not remodelling: broad groups are the right currency for headline cross-programme comparison, but equity monitoring should retain the disaggregated view, because the groups with the clearest signals in these data are precisely the ones the broad categories average away.

3.4 Deprivation

The deprivation gradient suggests babies from more deprived areas have higher odds of NCC admission:

Babies from the most deprived quintile have 2% higher odds of NCC admission than the least deprived, and the gradient steps up consistently across quintiles. Two interpretations are worth separating explicitly, because they carry different implications:

  • Deprivation as a proxy for unmeasured need (the most likely reading). Our clinical adjustment — gestation, and in the sensitivity model birth weight and Apgar — almost certainly does not capture the full excess morbidity carried by babies from the most deprived areas. On this reading the gradient is the model responding correctly to higher need that our covariates only partly measure; it is consistent with equitable, need-driven admission rather than evidence of a problem. Supporting this, the gradient is essentially unchanged after birth weight and Apgar are added (see the sensitivity comparison below) — what we would expect if it reflects residual, unmeasured need.
  • A genuine difference in admission propensity. Alternatively, hospitals serving deprived populations may admit at a lower threshold, or high-risk pregnancies may be concentrated in specialist centres in urban/deprived areas. If any part of the gradient reflects a true difference in access rather than need, that would warrant separate investigation.

These cannot be fully separated with the current model, but there is a direct check available: if morbidity itself rises across IMD quintiles in this cohort — and especially if it rises in ways the model does not adjust for — the unmeasured-need reading is supported.

3.4.2 Does the deprivation gradient differ by ethnic group?

The models so far constrain the deprivation gradient to be the same in every ethnic group (and equivalently, ethnicity gaps to be the same in every deprivation quintile). A natural follow-up question is whether the two dimensions of inequality compound — for example, whether deprivation carries a steeper penalty for some ethnic groups than others.

To test this, the primary model was refitted with a full ethnicity × deprivation interaction. The headline result: there is a statistically detectable difference in gradient steepness across groups, but it is small enough that the simpler, single-gradient model remains the right one to use — the detail behind that judgement is below.

This is presented as exploratory: the interaction adds 36 parameters, and picking through 36 individual cell contrasts would invite exactly the multiple-comparison problem flagged elsewhere in this report. The primary readout is therefore a single global likelihood-ratio test of whether there is evidence the gradient differs at all, which is one test and carries no multiplicity burden. The global test does find evidence that the gradient is not uniform: χ²(36) = 65.7, p = 0.0018. But the departure is small. Both information criteria prefer the simpler main-effects model (AIC +6, BIC +405 for the interaction model): with 476,475 births, a likelihood-ratio test is powered to detect departures from uniformity far too small to matter practically. The main-effects model remains the primary specification.

The pattern behind the global signal (Figure 5) is informative, read descriptively. The only within-group gradient whose interval excludes 1 is White British (OR 1.13, 95% CI 1.08–1.18) — slightly steeper than the pooled estimate of 1.1. Several minority groups have point estimates of similar or larger magnitude (Indian, Pakistani, Black), but with intervals spanning 1; others (Bangladeshi, Chinese, Other Asian) point flat or below, with very wide intervals. The imprecision is structural rather than incidental: minority ethnic births are concentrated in the more deprived quintiles, so the least-deprived reference cells are small — 306 Bangladeshi and 376 Chinese births in the least deprived quintile, against tens of thousands of White British.

Two conclusions follow. First, there is no evidence that deprivation carries a steeper penalty for minority ethnic groups — the detectable gradient sits within the White British majority, and the pooled deprivation OR reported by the primary model is dominated by that group. Second, that is a statement about the limits of the data as much as about the world: for the smaller groups the data cannot distinguish a flat gradient from a substantial one in either direction, so absence of evidence here is not evidence of absence. Neither conclusion disturbs the unmeasured-need reading of the main deprivation effect, and the main-effects model remains the primary specification throughout.

3.5 Hospital Variation

Once gestation, ethnicity and deprivation are controlled for, the trust a baby is born in is the largest remaining source of variation in the model: the ICC of 0.317 dwarfs the ethnicity ORs (typically 0.87–1.05) and the deprivation OR (1.08 for most vs least deprived). This should not be read simply as “where a baby is born matters most”: the ICC bundles together at least four things this dataset cannot fully separate:

  1. Genuine practice variation — different clinical thresholds for NCC admission between hospitals.
  2. Reporting variation — MSDS submission completeness varies considerably between providers, and PbR-CC submission is also patchy. The dual-source NCC_Admitted flag mitigates this but does not eliminate it: nationally the CC source captures ~3.8x more NCC admissions than MSDS alone, and that ratio differs by provider.
  3. Case-mix concentration via antenatal referral — high-risk pregnancies are transferred in utero to tertiary centres, so babies born at NICU trusts carry more unmeasured risk than gestation alone records. (Postnatal transfers cannot inflate a delivery trust’s rate in this model: an admission is attributed to the trust where the baby was born, not where the care was given.)
  4. Residual case-mix — gestation alone is a thin proxy for clinical need. The sensitivity model (gestation + birth weight + Apgar) reduces but does not remove this.

The ICC is therefore a useful signal that something at the provider level is driving variation, but it should not be reported as a clean estimate of variation in admission practice. Where it has been possible to decompose it — the funnel-outlier diagnostic — the evidence points to reporting breadth (what gets counted as an NCC day) as the dominant driver at the extremes. Providers in the tails may warrant follow-up, but an extreme estimate could equally reflect submission completeness, referral patterns, local thresholds for late-preterm admission, or case-mix not captured by gestation alone.

5 Length of Stay Among Admitted Babies

The models above ask who gets NCC care; this section asks how much care admitted babies then receive. Unlike the timing analysis (Phase 2, on hold), length of stay does not depend on a birth datetime: the outcome is NCC_CC_Total_Days, a count of daily critical-care records, so it is available now.

Of the 57,072 admitted babies in the primary analysis sample, 53,090 (93%) have at least one CC daily record and form the length-of-stay sample. The remaining 3,982 are MSDS-only admissions with no CC records — their stay length is unobservable in this data, an exclusion to bear in mind if MSDS-only status is unevenly distributed across providers (it is; see the funnel section). Stay length is the total number of days with an NCC critical-care record within 90 days of birth, summed across care periods.

The distribution is heavily skewed — median 4 days (IQR 2–8), 99th percentile 86 days — and gestation dominates it: median stays run from 3 days at term to 61 days below 28 weeks. Any crude group comparison therefore mostly re-describes each group’s preterm mix.

5.1 What adjustment does to the crude picture

The Black crude excess is entirely case-mix. After gestation-band adjustment the rate ratio for Black babies is 0.98 (0.95–1.01) — the 25% crude excess in mean stay disappears entirely, because Black babies’ longer average stays reflect their higher preterm rates, not longer stays at a given severity. Combined with the admission model (OR ~1.04) this extends the equitable-access finding: Black babies are admitted at parity and, once admitted, receive the expected quantity of care for their gestation.

Pakistani and Bangladeshi babies stay around 9–10% longer than expected. The two groups with the most interesting admission-model results are also the ones with adjusted stay excesses that survive BH correction: Pakistani 1.09 (1.05–1.12) and Bangladeshi 1.10 (1.04–1.16). For Pakistani babies the direction is notable: this group had ~11% lower adjusted odds of admission, and lower admission propensity combined with longer stays once admitted is the pattern one would expect if the effective admission threshold is higher — the marginal, milder cases don’t get admitted, so those who do are sicker and stay longer. This is a hypothesis, not a demonstration: greater within-gestation-band morbidity (e.g. growth restriction, congenital anomaly rates) would produce the same signature, and this model cannot separate the two. For Bangladeshi babies, whose admission odds were slightly above parity, the threshold reading does not fit, and residual severity is the more natural explanation. Either way, these are again the two groups that a broad “Asian” category would average away.

The deprivation gradient in stay length is almost entirely case-mix. The crude mean gap (10.5 vs 9.0 days, most vs least deprived) shrinks to a residual 3.2% (RR 1.03 (1.01–1.06)) — small, and consistent with the unmeasured-need reading: the model adjusts for gestation band but not for the growth restriction that rises with deprivation within gestation bands.

Trusts vary substantially in recorded stay length. The trust random-intercept SD (0.31 on the log scale) implies a trust one SD above average records ~36% longer stays than expected for its case-mix. This should not be read as pure clinical practice: the funnel diagnostic showed that what gets counted as an NCC day varies markedly between trusts, and that recording breadth flows directly into this outcome. The stay-length measure here is “days carrying an NCC critical-care record”, not independently verified cot-days.

WarningReading length of stay conditional on admission

This analysis conditions on admission, which is itself the outcome of the earlier models. If a group’s admission threshold differs, its admitted babies are a differently-selected slice of the same underlying population — which changes expected stay length without any difference in care received. Rate ratios here should therefore be read jointly with the admission model, never in isolation. The Pakistani finding above is the concrete example: its interpretation depends directly on the admission result.

6 Mortality Among Live Births: A Descriptive Look

The final feedback item asks about outcomes. This section is deliberately descriptive — no regression model, and heavier caveats than elsewhere in this report — because death ascertainment in this extract is partial in ways that could differ between groups. MBRRACE-UK, with linked death registrations, is the authoritative source on neonatal mortality inequalities; the purpose here is the narrower one of checking whether the mortality patterns visible in this data are consistent with the access findings above.

6.1 Measuring death in this data

Mirroring the dual-source design of the NCC admission flag, a baby is counted as having died if either source records it: the MSDS death date (deaths known to the maternity service), or — added in a July 2026 extract refresh — a hospital spell in APCS ending with discharge method “died”, which also captures deaths after maternity discharge in any hospital, up to the end of the extract window (30 April 2024). Among 504,392 live births, 1,448 deaths are recorded (2.87 per 1,000): 731 in both sources, 593 in APCS only and 124 in MSDS only. The two sources corroborate each other well — where both record a death, 685 of 731 (94%) agree on the date to within a day — but neither alone is complete: MSDS sees 59% of the combined total and APCS 91%. A further 45 babies recorded as live births in MSDS but coded as stillbirths in APCS were removed as vital-status-uncertain.

Three properties of this flag must be kept in view. First, it means “death recorded in hospital or by the maternity service within the extract window”, not 28-day neonatal mortality. With no day of birth available in this environment, age at death cannot be computed, so no clinically standard mortality window can be constructed. Second, follow-up is uneven: a baby born in March 2024 has around two months of in-window observation against thirteen for an April 2023 birth. In practice this matters little — recorded death rates are flat across birth months (2.6–3.1 per 1,000, with no decline at the end of the year), as expected when most in-hospital infant deaths occur close to birth. Third, deaths outside hospital after discharge are invisible here, and that invisibility need not be evenly distributed across groups.

The flag behaves as clinical reality would predict: rates run from 270 per 1,000 below 28 weeks’ gestation to 0.8 per 1,000 at term — a gradient of roughly 350-fold — and NCC-admitted babies die at 12.3 per 1,000 against 1.6 among the never-admitted.

6.3 What standardisation does to the crude picture

The Black crude excess is mostly gestational mix. The residual leans above 1 without reaching significance; MBRRACE, with full death-registration linkage and richer adjustment, does find a genuine residual excess for Black babies, so the near-parity here should be read as limited power and crude adjustment, not as contradicting it.

The Pakistani excess is not gestational mix. Pakistani babies show 80% more recorded deaths than their gestational-age distribution predicts (O/E 1.80 (1.50–2.15), BH-adjusted p < 0.001) — the strongest signal in this section, and consistent with MBRRACE findings of elevated perinatal mortality for babies of Pakistani ethnicity at every gestation, in which congenital anomalies are a documented contributor. Set alongside this report’s other Pakistani findings — around 11% lower adjusted odds of NCC admission, and around 9% longer stays once admitted — a consistent picture emerges of a group with higher within-gestation clinical need whose access does not appear correspondingly higher. All three signals point to the same follow-up: severity at admission (see Recommendation 6 in the Findings report).

The deprivation gradient survives standardisation. The most deprived quintile records 20% more deaths than its gestational mix predicts (O/E 1.20 (1.09–1.32)) and the least deprived 23% fewer (0.77 (0.64–0.92)), a monotone gradient across quintiles. As with the admission-model gradient, this is consistent with deprivation-linked morbidity within gestation bands (growth restriction rises with deprivation even among term babies — see the morbidity check).

One estimate is implausibly low. White Other babies record 37% fewer deaths than expected (O/E 0.63 (0.49–0.80), BH-adjusted p < 0.001). A genuinely lower risk is possible, but this group plausibly includes families with stronger ties abroad, and a death after leaving the country — or after transfer out of NHS hospital care — is invisible to both sources. This is a concrete reminder that ascertainment, as well as risk, can differ by group — which is why this section stays descriptive.

WarningWhat this mortality flag is not

This is not neonatal mortality as nationally defined: no age at death can be computed (no day of birth in this environment), deaths are only visible while in hospital or known to the maternity service, and the observation window is administrative (to 30 April 2024) rather than clinical (28 days). Group comparisons assume ascertainment is equal across groups, which the White Other result suggests may not hold. These figures are an internal consistency check on this dataset’s access findings — for population inequalities in neonatal mortality, MBRRACE-UK perinatal surveillance is the appropriate reference.

7 Limitations

  1. Clinical covariate completeness: Birth weight is recorded for ~46% of births and Apgar for ~79%; jointly they limit the sensitivity model to under half the cohort. The primary model uses gestation alone to preserve sample size.

  2. Single year of data: Results may not generalise to other time periods.

  3. Residual confounding: While ethnicity is now disaggregated (e.g., Indian, Pakistani, Bangladeshi separately), within-group heterogeneity remains. Black ethnicities are still grouped together in the model.

  4. Selection bias: Analysis restricted to births with complete data (94% of all births) — though the retained share is high and similar across providers (see the funnel diagnostic).

  5. Outcome definition: “NCC admission” is whatever providers record as NCC in two administrative sources, and the funnel diagnostic shows the breadth of that recording varies materially between providers. The outcome is best read as “received care recorded as NCC”, not “occupied a critical care cot”.
